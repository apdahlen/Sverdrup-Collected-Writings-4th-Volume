STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards:

* “Diakonisesagen / Diakonisegjerningen” – denotes the organized Lutheran deaconess movement, not merely charity or nursing.
* “Forstanderinde” – office-bearing head of an institution; stronger than “matron.”
* “Prøvesøstre” – probationary sisters; term carries ecclesial and vocational nuance.
* “Krediter” – here means supporters or backers, not financial credit in the modern sense.
* “istå” – archaic spelling of “i stå,” meaning to come to a standstill.

Syntactic Hazards:

* Long narrative sentences with multiple coordinated clauses must not be broken down excessively.
* Passive constructions and formal sequencing reflect institutional seriousness.
* Chronological flow is embedded within sentence structure.

Theological Hazards:

* “Diakonisegjerningen” carries theological weight as a calling and ministry, not a mere organization.
* Language of perseverance (“skulde ikke dø bort”) echoes providential framing.

Voice Hazards:

* The restrained but earnest institutional tone must be preserved.
* Avoid modern nonprofit or managerial diction.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* “Veien til at faa … igang” – idiomatic for the proper or God-ordained means of beginning a work.
* “tage fat igjen” – to take hold again; implies renewed resolve, not mere resumption.
* “skulde ikke dø bort” – providential idiom suggesting divine preservation rather than human optimism.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

* Setting: Minneapolis, General Council meeting, autumn 1888.
* Catalyst: Conversation among clergy and leaders.
* Initial action: Calling of a deaconess and formation of committee.
* Crisis: Leader’s departure, stagnation, loss of support.
* Resolution: Renewal, incorporation, formal organization.
* Conclusion: Stable institutional governance established.

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* Implicit providence language (“skulde ikke dø bort”).
* Vocation and calling motifs consistent with Acts-style church organization.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* Maintain seriousness without embellishment.
* Let inevitability and perseverance carry moral weight.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* Preserve necessity and resolve implied in “skulde ikke.”
* Avoid weakening to mere possibility.

STEP 7: CONSISTENT THEOLOGICAL TERM RENDERING

* Diakonisegjerningen → “the deaconess work.”
* Forstanderinde → “Superintendent.”
* Søstre → “Sisters.”

STEP 8: METAPHOR INTEGRITY PRESERVATION

* Preserve life/death imagery regarding the work’s survival.

STEP 9: TONE CONTRAST PRESERVATION

* Contrast between collapse and renewal must remain clear.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* Retain long narrative sentences suitable for public reading.

STEP 11: ACADEMIC TRANSLATION (FINAL)

It was in the autumn of 1888, when the General Council held its meeting in Minneapolis, that several brethren gathered in the home of Pastor M. Jallf Gjertsen at his invitation. Among the guests were the aged Dr. Passavant of Pittsburgh, Dr. N. Spaeth of Philadelphia, Pastor G. Høyme of Eau Claire, Wisconsin, and several others. Pastor Gjertsen directed the conversation toward the deaconess cause, raising the question of how it might best be set in motion in Minneapolis. Dr. Spaeth spoke at considerable length and with great warmth concerning the matter. Dr. Passavant remarked briefly and plainly that the way to set the deaconess cause in motion was to begin with a deaconess.

Very shortly thereafter, Deaconess Elisabeth Jedde of New York came to Minneapolis on a vacation journey. At that time she had labored for approximately five years in New York, whither she had come from the Norwegian Deaconess Home in Kristiania, Norway. After consultation with her, a committee was formed to begin an independent deaconess work in Minneapolis. The committee consisted of Pastors Gjertsen and N. Iversen, Professor S. Ostedal, and Messrs. R. Sunde, L. Swenson, N. A. Nilsen, and S. Bottelsen. Banker R. Sunde offered the free use of a house on Hennepin Avenue. The committee called Sister Elisabeth Jedde as Superintendent and gathered the necessary funds for the furnishing of a home for the Sisters and for a hospital. Two young women presented themselves as probationary Sisters, and on the first of November 1888 the Superintendent and one of the probationary Sisters moved into the house, and the work began.

Before long, however, difficulties also made their appearance. The Superintendent was urgently requested to return to New York in order, if possible, to resolve complications that had arisen there in the hospital and deaconess work which she had directed before coming to Minneapolis. She departed and remained in New York for a considerable time. The new work in Minneapolis came to a standstill. Of the three admitted Sisters, one withdrew, the other two remained without occupation, the treasury was empty, and several members of the committee resigned.

But the deaconess work was not to die out in Minneapolis in this manner. Some of the members of the former committee gathered once more for a meeting; new supporters were added; and it was resolved to take hold again. The Superintendent returned from New York in the month of July 1889. It was decided to form an incorporated association under the name Norwegian Lutheran Deaconess Institute for the advancement of the cause. Of this association, whose interests are governed by a Board of Trustees consisting of nine members, anyone may become a member by paying one dollar per year or ten dollars once for all.* The association was incorporated under the laws of Minnesota on the seventeenth of August 1889. The first Board of Trustees consisted of Pastors M. Jallf Gjertsen, M. Hegge, and G. Høyme; Professor S. Ostedal; Professor G. Everdrup; Engineer Olaf Hoff; Deaconess Elisabeth Jedde; Mr. John J. Hove; and Mr. L. Swenson. Professor Everdrup was elected chairman of the Board, an office to which he has since been re-elected each year.

* This provision has now been altered such that one may become a member of the Deaconess Association only by paying ten dollars once for all. — Ed.
